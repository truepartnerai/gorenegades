## Blog

Somewhere out there is a customer who thinks you're wonderful. They told their spouse about you. They said "those guys were great" at a neighborhood barbecue, probably with a paper plate in one hand. They fully intend to leave you a five-star review, and in their mind they have more or less already done it.

They haven't, and they won't. It's nothing personal.

I say this with affection, because I am that customer. I have promised glowing reviews to a dentist, a mechanic, at least two restaurants, and the man who revived our sprinkler system during a July that felt like a personal attack. My intentions were pure. My follow-through lived somewhere between "later" and "never," which, if we're being honest, are the same zip code.

## Nobody forgot you on purpose

What's actually happening is boring, which is good news, because boring problems can be fixed.

A customer is happiest right after the job is done. The house is clean, the car starts, the website finally loads, the tax return got filed without anybody crying. That is exactly when they'd leave a glowing review. It's also exactly when they're paying you, walking to their car, and answering a text from their kid about who's picking up whom from practice. The warm feeling fades by dinner. By Thursday you're a pleasant memory. By next month you're competing for their attention with a password reset and a notice from the HOA.

So most businesses end up with a review profile that is a very strange sample of reality: a handful of people who loved them enough to go hunting for the link, plus the one guy who was upset about parking. Meanwhile the next prospect is standing in their kitchen scrolling Google, comparing you to a competitor with a couple hundred reviews who is not actually better than you. They're just better at asking.

## The ask is easy. Remembering is the hard part.

Most owners know they should ask for reviews. The trouble is that the ask depends on a human remembering to do it, at the right moment, every single time, forever. That's a lot to expect from yourself on the Tuesday when two people call in sick. It's even more to expect from your team, who were hired to fix furnaces or cut hair or close loans, and not to run a reputation program in their spare time.

This is the kind of job people are reliably bad at and software is reliably good at. It's small, it's repetitive, and it's time-sensitive, which is the holy trinity of tasks that fall through the cracks.

## What a review system actually looks like

It is much less impressive than it sounds, and that is the whole point. When a job is marked done (the deal moves to "closed," the invoice gets paid, the appointment ends), a text goes out within the hour. It's short and human: thanks for having us out, and if we took good care of you, would you mind leaving a quick review? The link drops them straight into the review box, instead of on your homepage, where they'd have to find it like the last clue in an escape room.

If they don't get to it, a friendly reminder goes out a couple of days later, and then the system goes quiet. Nobody gets nagged into resentment.

Inside Renegade CRM, that's a review request attached to a simple workflow, set up once. After that, every customer gets asked at the moment they're most likely to say yes, including all the ones your team would have forgotten. New reviews land in one place so you can reply to them, and the good ones make excellent social posts, which is a whole other article.
